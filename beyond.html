Beyond the lecture, for the curious.

K-means and hierarchical clustering are the foundations. Two more methods fix their best-known weaknesses: DBSCAN finds clusters of any shape and labels outliers as noise, and Gaussian mixture models handle stretched clusters and give soft, probabilistic memberships. Then we put every method on every dataset, side by side.

01DBSCAN: clusters by density

DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) says a cluster is a dense region of points, separated from other clusters by sparse regions. It needs two settings instead of K:

  • \(\varepsilon\) (eps): the radius of a point's neighbourhood.
  • min_samples: how many points (including itself) must fall inside that radius to count as "dense".
●

Core point

Has at least min_samples points within \(\varepsilon\). Clusters grow from core points.

○

Border point

Not core itself, but within \(\varepsilon\) of a core point. Joins that cluster.

✕

Noise

Neither core nor near a core point. Left unclustered: an outlier.

DBSCAN playground

Hover a point to see its ε-neighbourhood.

k-distance plot: how to choose ε

Each point's distance to its min_samples-th nearest neighbour, sorted. A good ε sits at the knee, where the curve shoots up: below it are points inside clusters, above it are outliers. The dashed line is your current ε.

Strengths

  • No K: the number of clusters is discovered
  • Any shape: moons, rings, snakes
  • Labels outliers as noise instead of forcing them into a cluster

Weaknesses

  • One ε for the whole dataset: struggles when clusters have different densities
  • Sensitive to ε; needs scaled features
  • Distances lose meaning in high dimensions (reduce with PCA first)

02Gaussian mixtures: soft clusters

A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) assumes the data was generated by K bell-shaped (Gaussian) clouds, each with its own centre, shape and size. It is model-based and soft: every point gets a probability of belonging to each cluster, like the 70% student / 30% family customer from the overview.

It is fitted with the EM algorithm, which looks a lot like K-means:

E-step · like "assign"

Expectation

For every point, compute the probability that each Gaussian produced it (its responsibilities). Soft, not hard.

M-step · like "update"

Maximisation

Re-estimate each Gaussian's mean, covariance (shape) and weight from the points, weighted by their responsibilities.

EM, step by step

Ellipses show 1 and 2 standard deviations. Colours blend by probability. Hover a point.

Log-likelihood (higher is better)

K-means is a special case of a GMM dive deeper

Force every Gaussian to be round with the same tiny variance, give every cluster the same weight, and turn the soft responsibilities into hard 0/1 choices: the E-step becomes "join the nearest centre" and the M-step becomes "move to the mean". That is exactly K-means. A GMM relaxes all three assumptions, which is why it can fit stretched and unequal clusters. Like K-means, EM finds a local optimum, so libraries run several starts (n_init) and keep the highest likelihood. Try New start on the stretched blobs until you see a bad one.

04Which method should I use?

Your situationTry firstWhy
Round, similar-sized groups; lots of dataK-meansFast, simple, interpretable centroids
You want to see the structure at every scale, or explain it with a treeHierarchical (average / Ward)The dendrogram is the explanation
Curved or snake-like shapes, outliers presentDBSCANDensity-based; labels noise
Stretched or overlapping groups; you need probabilitiesGaussian mixtureFlexible shapes, soft memberships
Many columns (say 20+)PCA first, then clusterRemoves noise and fights the curse of dimensionality
You just want to look at high-dimensional dataPCA to 2D; t-SNE / UMAP for curved structureVisualisation, not clustering
Whatever you choose

Scale your features, try more than one method and setting, and judge the result with a domain expert. In unsupervised learning, "sensible and useful" is the only test that really counts.

05Where to go next

visualisation

t-SNE and UMAP

Non-linear methods that place similar points close together in 2D. Great for pictures of complex data (like the twisted ring PCA could not flatten), but distances and cluster sizes in the picture can mislead.

non-linear PCA

Kernel PCA

Runs PCA in a transformed space, so it can unroll some curved structures.

deep learning

Autoencoders

Neural networks that squeeze data through a small "bottleneck" layer and rebuild it: a non-linear cousin of PCA compression.

density

HDBSCAN and OPTICS

Successors of DBSCAN that cope with clusters of different densities.

evaluation

Cluster validity

Silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and Calinski–Harabasz scores; adjusted Rand index when labels exist.

applications

Anomaly detection

Isolation forests and one-class methods: find the rows that do not belong anywhere.
