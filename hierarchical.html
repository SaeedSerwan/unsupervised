Part 1 · Clustering · Hierarchical

Hierarchical clustering, grown like a tree.

Instead of one set of K groups, hierarchical clustering builds a tree called a dendrogram. It shows which points are most alike, which small groups join into bigger ones, and so on up to a single root. You do not choose K first: you build the tree, look at it, then decide where to cut.

01A family tree of the data

Picture a family tree upside down. At the bottom, every leaf is one data point. Going up, two very similar points join first, low down. Then small groups join other groups. At the top, everything joins into one big cluster: the root.

The one rule

The height where two things join tells you how different they are. Join low: very similar. Join high: very different.

The big advantage over K-means: we do not have to choose \(K\) first. One tree gives every possible number of clusters, from \(n\) (each point alone) down to 1. In medicine this is popular because the tree itself is a picture you can show a doctor: tumour samples clustered by gene activity, with the subtypes visible as branches.

02Reading a dendrogram

A common mistake: two leaves that sit next to each other at the bottom of the tree look similar. They may not be. The left-to-right order of leaves is partly arbitrary (you can flip any branch without changing the tree). Only the height of the fusion matters.

03Cutting the tree

To get actual groups, draw a horizontal line across the tree. Every branch the line crosses becomes one cluster. Cut high: few clusters. Cut low: many. This is why we do not need K in advance, but we still decide where to cut (K in disguise).

Drag the cut line

Drag the dashed line on the tree, or use the slider.

Where should I cut? dive deeper

Look for a long vertical gap: a range of heights where no merges happen. Cutting inside that gap gives groups that are far apart compared with how tight they are. In the lab, 3 blobs show one obvious gap; "no structure" data has none, and every cut is equally arbitrary. You can also use the silhouette score from the K-means lesson on each cut, or a domain reason ("the business wants 4 segments").

04Building it bottom-up

The usual method is agglomerative ("agglomerate" means to gather into a ball): start with every point alone and keep joining the two closest clusters.

  1. Start with \(n\) clusters, one point each. Compute the distance between every pair.
  2. Repeat for \(i = n, n-1, \dots, 2\):
    • Find the two clusters that are closest and merge them. Their distance is the height of the join in the tree.
    • Update the distances between the new cluster and all the others.

Each loop reduces the number of clusters by one, so after \(n-1\) merges we reach the root.

05Linkage: the distance between groups

The distance between two points is easy (Euclidean). The distance between two groups has no single answer. Do we use the two closest points? The two farthest? The average? The rule we choose is called the linkage.

LinkageDistance between groups A and BTypical result
Completethe two farthest points: \(\max_{a\in A,\, b\in B} d(a,b)\)compact, balanced clusters
Singlethe two closest points: \(\min_{a\in A,\, b\in B} d(a,b)\)long "chains"; can follow curved shapes
Averagethe average of all \(|A|\cdot|B|\) point-to-point distancesbalanced; a good default
Centroiddistance between the two group centres \(\bar{x}_A\) and \(\bar{x}_B\)can produce "inversions" in the tree

What does each linkage measure?

Drag any point. The dashed lines show which distance is used.

In practice, complete and average linkage are the most popular because they give balanced trees. scikit-learn's default is Ward linkage, which merges the pair of clusters that increases the total within-cluster variation the least: the K-means objective, applied bottom-up.

Four linkages side by side

Lesson

Always try more than one linkage and check whether the groups make sense. On blobs, complete, average and Ward agree. On curved shapes, single linkage wins because it follows chains of close neighbours. With noise, the same chaining makes single linkage glue everything together.

07How big can it get?

Hierarchical clustering needs the distance between every pair of points. That number grows with the square of \(n\).

For huge data, K-means is usually the practical choice: each iteration only compares every point with K centroids.

08Pros, cons, and K-means

Pros

  • No K needed up front
  • The tree is a picture you can show a client or a doctor
  • One run gives every number of clusters
  • Deterministic: no random start

Cons

  • You still choose where to cut (K in disguise)
  • Results depend on distance and linkage
  • Needs all pairwise distances: slow and memory-hungry for large n
  • Merges are final: an early mistake is never undone
