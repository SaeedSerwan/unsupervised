Part 2 · Dimensionality reduction

Principal component analysis, fewer columns, same story.

PCA finds a few new directions in the data that keep as much of its spread (variance) as possible. Use them as new columns and you can plot 50-dimensional data in 2D, compress it, or speed up other models. This lesson builds the intuition by rotating lines and point clouds, then teaches you how to decide how many components to keep, with many datasets to practise on.

01Why fewer columns?

1

We cannot see 50 columns

We can plot 2 or 3. To look at high-dimensional data, we must squeeze it.

2

The curse of dimensionality

With many features, points drift far apart, distances lose meaning, and models need far more data. See it here.

3

Redundant columns

Height in cm and arm span in cm say almost the same thing. One combined column can replace both.

We have met dimensionality reduction before in the supervised setting: lasso and forward/backward selection keep some columns and drop others, guided by \(y\). Today there is no \(y\). So instead of choosing columns, PCA builds new columns that are mixtures of the old ones.

03Variance is information

PCA looks for a few new directions that keep as much of the spread (variance) as possible. Why variance? Because that is where the information is. A column where every value is the same tells you nothing; a column whose values vary a lot helps tell the rows apart.

Very practical warning

Always centre each column first (subtract its mean), and usually scale it too (divide by its standard deviation). Otherwise a column in large units looks "important" just because its numbers are big. More in §07.

04The first principal component

The first principal component \(Z_1\) is a new column made by mixing the old ones:

$$Z_1 = \phi_{11}X_1 + \phi_{21}X_2 + \dots + \phi_{p1}X_p \qquad \text{with} \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_{j1}^2 = 1$$

The weights \(\phi\) are the loadings: how much each original feature contributes. We pick them so that \(Z_1\) has the biggest possible variance. The constraint (squares add to 1) fixes the length of the weight vector: without it we could make the variance as big as we like just by using huge weights. So only the direction matters.

Rotate a line through the cloud

Drag on the plot or use the slider. Every point drops onto the line; watch the spread of the dropped points.

Variance of the projections as the line turns

Two views of the same line

The direction that maximises the spread of the dropped points is also the direction that minimises the total squared length of the drop lines. PC1 is both the "most informative" direction and the line that hugs the data most closely. Watch both numbers in the lab: one peaks exactly where the other bottoms out.

Example: if \(Z_1 = 0.7 \times \text{height} + 0.7 \times \text{weight}\), then \(Z_1\) is basically "body size". Each person's value on it, \(z_{i1}\), is called their score.

05The same idea as maths

Find the weights that make the new scores spread out as much as possible, while keeping the weights' length fixed:

$$\underset{\phi_{11},\dots,\phi_{p1}}{\text{maximize}} \left\{ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_{j1}x_{ij}\Big)^{2} \right\} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_{j1}^{2}=1$$
\(n,\ p\)
number of rows (observations) and columns (features)
\(x_{ij}\)
the (centred) value in row \(i\), column \(j\)
\(z_{i1}=\sum_j \phi_{j1}x_{ij}\)
the score of row \(i\) on PC1: where it lands on the line
\(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i z_{i1}^2\)
the variance of the scores (the data is centred, so the mean is 0)

We do not solve this by hand. It has an exact answer from linear algebra: the loadings are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix (see §12).

06PC2 and beyond

After \(Z_1\), we want a second direction that adds new information. So \(Z_2\) is the direction with the most remaining variance that is uncorrelated with \(Z_1\). Geometrically: it is at a right angle (orthogonal) to PC1. \(Z_3\) is orthogonal to both, and so on. With \(p\) features there are at most \(p\) components.

08How many components? 3D → 2D → 1D

This is the question students ask most. With \(p\) features you can keep anywhere from 1 to \(p\) components. Keep too few and you lose information; keep too many and you have not simplified anything. The answer depends on the shape of the data, which the proportion of variance explained (PVE) by each component reveals.

$$\text{PVE}_m = \frac{\lambda_m}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_p} \qquad \text{cumulative PVE}(k) = \sum_{m=1}^{k}\text{PVE}_m$$

Here \(\lambda_m\) is the variance along component \(m\). Pick a dataset, choose how many components to keep, and see what survives. Each dataset teaches a different lesson.

09A recipe for choosing the number of components

There is no single correct number, but there is a reliable routine. Use two or three rules together and let your purpose break ties.

step 1

Standardise

Unless all columns share the same units and you want big-variance columns to count more.

step 2

Fit all components

PCA() with no n_components, then read explained_variance_ratio_.

step 3

Draw the scree plot

Bars for PVE, a line for cumulative PVE.

step 4

Apply the rules

Cumulative threshold, elbow and Kaiser (below). When they agree, you are done.

step 5

Let the purpose decide

Plotting: 2 or 3. Feeding a model: cross-validate k. Compressing: pick the error you can accept.

step 6

Interpret

Read the loadings. If a component means something ("size", "science ability"), that is a good sign.

RuleHow it worksStrengthWeakness
Cumulative thresholdSmallest k with cumulative PVE ≥ 80%, 90% or 95%Simple, easy to explainThe threshold is arbitrary; on noisy data it can demand many PCs
Elbow (scree)Keep the components before the curve flattensVisual, adapts to the dataElbows can be vague or appear twice
KaiserKeep components with eigenvalue \(\lambda > 1\) (standardised data only)Automatic: a PC must explain more than one original column's worthTends to keep too many when p is large
Purpose2–3 for plots; cross-validation for prediction; error budget for compressionAnswers the real questionNeeds a clear goal

In scikit-learn you can ask for the threshold directly: PCA(n_components=0.90) keeps as many components as needed to explain 90% of the variance.

12Under the hood: eigenvectors

For centred data \(X\) (n × p), the covariance matrix is \(\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X\). Its eigenvectors and eigenvalues give everything PCA needs:

$$\Sigma\,\phi_m = \lambda_m\,\phi_m, \qquad \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_p \ge 0$$
  • The eigenvector \(\phi_m\) holds the loadings of component \(m\) (a direction of length 1).
  • The eigenvalue \(\lambda_m\) is the variance of the scores along that direction, so \(\text{PVE}_m = \lambda_m / \sum_j \lambda_j\).
  • Eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal: that is why PCs are at right angles and their scores are uncorrelated.
  • The scores are \(Z = X\Phi\); keeping the first k columns of \(\Phi\) is the reduction, and \(X \approx Z_k\Phi_k^\top\) is the reconstruction.
SVD: how libraries actually compute it dive deeper

scikit-learn does not form \(\Sigma\). It computes the singular value decomposition \(X = U S V^\top\) of the centred data directly, which is more accurate. The columns of \(V\) are the loadings, the scores are \(US\), and the variances are \(\lambda_m = s_m^2/(n-1)\). Same answer, better numerics.

Also note that the sign of a component is arbitrary: \(\phi\) and \(-\phi\) describe the same line. Two programs may show your plot mirrored; nothing is wrong.

13Limits and pitfalls

PCA is great when

  • Features are correlated (redundant)
  • You need a 2D or 3D picture of many columns
  • You want fewer, uncorrelated inputs for another model
  • You need a fast, deterministic method

Watch out for

  • Different units: standardise first
  • Curved structure: PCA only finds flat directions (dataset G, the twisted ring)
  • Outliers: they inflate variance and pull the axes
  • Interpretation: PCs are mixtures and may have no clear meaning
  • Max variance ≠ best for prediction: a low-variance PC can still matter for y

For curved structure, non-linear methods such as kernel PCA, t-SNE and UMAP exist; they are mainly used for visualisation. See where to go next.
